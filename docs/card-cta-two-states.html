HIGHBASE · Special Price Request & RFQ

One slot,
two states.

The entry point on the product card is not one control — it is a control with a life. Before, it invites the buyer to ask. After, the same slot has to say a request is open, and give them the way back to it (AC-1.5). Twenty options, each drawn in both states — ten placements first, then ten icon-only marks that have to do the same job without a word.

What separates them is not where the button sits. It is whether the placement can still carry SPR-2608-0001 once the buyer has asked — because the reference is the one thing that says which request is open, and most of these have nowhere to put it.

Which ones survive being used

If you pick one

04 — Under the price. It is the only layout that holds the whole sentence in both states, keeps the reference on the control itself, and does not move anything on the card when the state flips — measured at 482px before and after. The buyer returns to the exact spot they left from, and it now says which request is waiting.

If you pick two

07 + 04. The ribbon is the best state signal on the board — a buyer scanning twenty cards sees which ones they have already asked about without reading — but it cannot carry the reference. Pair it with a placement that can, and each does the job it is good at.

What to avoid

09. Swapping one unlabelled glyph for another is not a state change a buyer will notice, and there is nowhere to put the reference. 05 fails the same test more quietly: Ask to Asked is a single letter, and the moment you add the reference line the layout has spent the height it was chosen to save.

Options 01–04 are live in the prototype and switchable from the demo bar; their requested states are the real ones. 05–10 are drawn here against the shipped tokens and need no new components — each is a rearrangement of the button, the band and the media chip the card already owns.

Part two · 11–20

Ten marks, no words

Option 09 asked whether one glyph welded to Add to cart could carry the whole feature. These ten answer it. The placement is fixed — every one uses 09’s segment — so the only variable is the mark itself, and whether it survives losing its label.

Each is drawn twice: the ask, and what the same segment becomes once a request is open. The large pair at the top of each plate is the mark at 26px so you can judge the drawing; the cards below are at true size, which is where it actually has to work.

What a mark can and cannot say

Read down the “reads as” column and the problem is plain. Every glyph here can say price. Most can say lower. Not one of them, unaided, says ask the supplier and wait for an answer — and none but 19 can carry the reference that identifies the request afterwards.

Best of the ten

17 — bubble with a falling price. It is the only mark whose requested state explains itself: a bubble with a dot reads as an open thread with something in it, which is exactly what an open request is. Every other option answers “you asked” with a fill or a tick and leaves the way back unstated.

Most accurate, least usable

16 — two-way arrows. It draws the counter-offer loop precisely, and it is also the standard mark for sort, swap and sync. On a labelled control that ambiguity costs nothing. On an unlabelled one it is fatal.

The honest conclusion

Icon-only does not carry this action. 19 only works with a pointer, 20 only works for buyers who were there on launch day, and 13 and 15 promise a discount the supplier has not agreed to. If the card must save the width, 02 from part one — the same mark plus the single word Request — costs nothing at all until a request is open, and 27px after. That is the price of being understood.
